# Whist, Ace-Partner Whist and Hjerterfri — complete rules

> **About this document.** Three distinct card games are described here. They are
> frequently confused with one another, but they share neither their objective, their
> deck composition, nor their partnership structure.
> The description rests exclusively on the sources stored in `sources/`.
> Where the sources are silent or contradict each other, this is stated explicitly
> rather than filled in with guesswork. See **[Open questions](#open-questions)** at
> the end.
>
> Danish edition: [`REGLER.da.md`](REGLER.da.md). The two documents are kept
> structurally parallel so they can be compared section by section.
>
> **A note on names.** Danish game and bid names are kept in Danish throughout, with
> an English gloss on first use. They are terms of art with no established English
> equivalents, and translating them would make the document useless at an actual
> table. *Es-makker whist* means "ace-partner whist"; *hjerterfri* is the Danish
> version of the game known internationally as *Hearts*.

## The three games at a glance

| | Classic whist | Es-makker whist | Hjerterfri |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Players** | 4 | 4 | 3–7 (4 is best) |
| **Deck** | 52 | **55** (52 + 3 jokers) | 52, no jokers |
| **Cards per player** | 13 | 13 | 13 (with four) |
| **Kitty / talon** | No | **Yes — 3 cards** | No |
| **Bidding round** | No | Yes | No |
| **Partnerships** | Fixed, sitting opposite | Shifting, concealed, formed by a called ace | None — every player for themselves |
| **Trump** | Last card dealt is turned up | Chosen, or locked by the bid | **None** |
| **Objective** | Win tricks | Win (or avoid) tricks per the bid | **Avoid** penalty cards |
| **Resolution** | Points per deal | Zero-sum points per deal | First to 100 points **loses** |

The essential difference: in classic whist the partnership is fixed in advance and the
partners sit opposite one another. In es-makker whist the partnership forms only
during play, determined by whoever happens to hold the called ace — so partners rarely
sit opposite each other. In hjerterfri there are no partnerships at all.

## Shared fundamentals

These terms mean the same thing in all three games and are not repeated under each one.

**Trick.** Each player plays one card. The completed round of cards is a trick. The
winner of the trick collects it and leads to the next one.

**Lead.** The first card of a trick. Its suit is the *led suit* and determines what
the others must follow.

**Following suit.** If you can follow the led suit, you **must**. If you cannot, you
are *void* in that suit and may then play whatever you like — trump or discard —
subject to whatever restrictions the individual game imposes.

**Winning a trick.** The highest card of the led suit takes the trick — unless someone
has trumped, in which case the highest trump takes it. Cards in suits other than the
led suit and trump can never take a trick, however high they are.

**Card ranking.** Ace high, 2 low: `A K Q J 10 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2`.

**Direction of play.** Clockwise.

**Dealer and eldest hand.** The dealer deals. *Eldest hand* is the player to the
dealer's left — the next player clockwise. Eldest hand bids first and leads first.

## Part 1 — Classic whist

The original English game, from which all the other variants descend. It is barely
played in Denmark today, but you need to know it in order to see what is distinctive
about the Danish development.

### Setup

- Four players, 52 cards, 13 cards each, 13 tricks per deal.
- **Fixed partnerships, partners sitting opposite one another.** The pairing is
  settled before the cards are dealt and does not change during play.

### Trump

There is **no bidding round**. The trump suit is determined by the **last card dealt**,
which is turned face up for the table. That suit is trump for the whole deal.

### Play

1. Eldest hand leads.
2. Follow suit. If you cannot, you may trump or discard.
3. The highest card of the led suit takes the trick, unless someone has trumped.
4. The winner of the trick leads to the next.
5. After 13 tricks the deal is scored.

### Related variants

| Variant | Players | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Auction whist** | 4 | Fixed partnerships, but trump is settled by bidding. The direct precursor to bridge |
| **Two-handed whist** | 2 | Adapted version without partnerships |
| **Three-handed whist** | 3 | One against two, often with rotating roles |

### Table talk

No information about your hand may be conveyed — not by word, expression, tone of
voice, or by hesitating before you play. **All communication between partners happens
through the cards that are played.** This is not a formality; it is the entire tactical
basis of the game, and it applies just as strictly in es-makker whist.

## Part 2 — Es-makker whist

The game most Danes mean when they say "whist". The description below follows
**Whistklubben's ruleset** (see [Sources](#sources)) and is therefore one specific
club's rules, not a national standard. Points that are known to vary between circles
are marked 🔶.

### 2.1 Deck and the deal

The game uses **55 cards**: a standard deck plus **three jokers**. The arithmetic
works out as:

```
4 players × 13 cards = 52
the kitty             =  3
                      ----
total                 = 55
```

Cards are dealt 13 to each player, **always clockwise** around the table.

### 2.2 The kitty ("katten")

During the deal, **3 cards** are set aside one at a time. These form the exchange
pile — colloquially **katten**, "the cat" — placed face down in the middle of the
table.

For the deal to be legal, two requirements apply:

1. Only **one card may go to the kitty at a time** — a card must be dealt to one of
   the 4 player hands before another card may go to the kitty.
2. **The very last card may never end up in the kitty.**

An illegal deal requires a reshuffle.

### 2.3 Reshuffling

- **Illegal deal** (breach of either requirement above) → reshuffle, and the deal is
  repeated.
- **Other misdeals**, if noticed and corrected so that the deal becomes legal →
  **no** reshuffle as a rule.
- A reshuffle may be *requested* at any time, provided it is done **before you have
  looked at your own cards**. Arguments in favour of a reshuffle are required.

### 2.4 Jernhånd ("iron hand")

**Jernhånd** is a declaration a player may invoke on being dealt 13 cards containing
**no court cards, no jokers and no aces**. The declaration restarts the deal. The
player may of course also choose to play the hand.

### 2.5 Exchanging with the kitty

> This is the point most often misunderstood. **The number of cards exchanged does not
> depend on the bid.** It is a fixed mechanic attached to the kitty.

The declarer — the winner of the bidding — may exchange **from 0 to 3** of their cards
with cards from the exchange pile.

- If the declarer does not take all three, **the player to the declarer's left** may
  exchange the remainder. If they decline, the remainder passes onward, and so on.
- **Exception — Halve:** here it is the partner who exchanges (see [2.9](#29-påhæng-bid-modifiers)).
- **Exception — sol-melding bids:** here the player who **went along last** has first right
  of exchange. If cards remain, the right passes to the second-to-last, and so on
  (see [2.10](#210-sol bids)).

### 2.6 The bidding

Bidding proceeds clockwise. **Eldest hand makes the first bid.** The next player must
then either *overcall* the bid or *pass*. If they pass, the turn moves on to the next
player, who either overcalls or passes.

When a bid is overcalled, **the player to the dealer's left must be given the chance to
overcall the new bid or pass** — the bidding, in other words, comes back around.

**A bid can only be overcalled by a higher bid.** The sole exception is the
*going along* mechanic, which applies to sol-melding bids and Vip.

**If everyone passes, the deal is restarted.**

### 2.7 "Going along" (gå med)

On sol-melding bids and Vip, a player who has **not yet passed** may choose to *go along* with
the bid instead of overcalling it.

Going along is treated as **an admission that you cannot overcall the bid**. The price
is that you **forfeit your place in the bidding**: if the bid is later overcalled by
another player, you cannot re-enter.

**The ruleset's example 1.**
Player 1 bids Ren sol. Player 2 goes along. Player 3 overcalls with 10+.
The bid returns to Player 1 — but Player 2 is out of the bidding.

**The ruleset's example 2.**
Player 1 bids 9. Player 2 overcalls with 9 Vip. Player 1 goes along with the vip.
Player 3 also goes along. Player 4 overcalls with 10. The bid returns to Player 2, who
started the 9 Vip — while Players 1 and 3 can no longer bid.

### 2.8 The bidding ladder

In the trick-taking bids, the bid states **how many tricks you undertake to win**. A
bid is either a plain number or a number with a *påhæng* (a modifier) that qualifies
it. The ruleset states that the numeric values run from 7 to 13, but the ladder as
set out begins at 8 🔶.

In order from lowest to highest (`+` denotes a modifier):

| # | Bid | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 | numeric |
| 2 | 8+ | numeric with modifier |
| 3 | 9 | numeric |
| 4 | **Sol** | sol-melding |
| 5 | 9+ | numeric with modifier |
| 6 | 10 | numeric |
| 7 | **Ren sol** | sol-melding |
| 8 | 10+ | numeric with modifier |
| 9 | 11 | numeric |
| 10 | **Bordlægger** | sol-melding |
| 11 | 11+ | numeric with modifier |
| 12 | 12 | numeric |
| 13 | **Ren bordlægger** | sol-melding |
| 14 | 12+ | numeric with modifier |
| 15 | 13 | numeric |
| 16 | 13+ | numeric with modifier |

Note the pattern: each sol-melding bid is wedged in **above** a plain number and **below**
that same number with a modifier. The sol-melding bids are therefore not a side category —
they compete directly in the same ladder as the numeric bids.

### 2.8.1 What beats what

**The main rule: number first, modifier second.** The modifier decides the ranking only
between two bids on the **same number**. A higher number always beats a lower number
with a modifier — 9 beats 8+, whichever modifier is involved.

> **Two different models.** The written source and the oral tradition do *not* describe
> the same system here. Both are given below, the source's last. This is the only part
> of the document where the two are directly irreconcilable.

#### Model A — tiers and sequence (oral tradition) 🔶

The modifiers do **not** sit in one long ranking. They are divided into **tiers** —
groups within which every modifier is equally strong. The rule has two parts:

1. **Between tiers, the higher tier always wins.** A modifier from a higher tier beats
   one from a lower tier, regardless of who bid first. **This holds without exception**
   and is the only thing in this entire section that does not vary locally.
2. **Within a tier, all modifiers are equal.** There, **the order of bidding** decides:
   the one bid later beats the one bid earlier.

It is the two parts together that create the impression that "some modifiers always
beat the others, while some are of equal standing". These are not two different rules —
it is one rule seen from two angles.

This mechanic exists **only** with modifiers. Two plain numeric bids can never overcall
each other — `9` does not overcall `9`, regardless of who said it first.

#### The tier assignment is local — and must be configuration

> ⚙️ **For implementation.** The mechanic above is fixed and can be coded as it stands.
> **The assignment below must not be hard-coded.** Which modifiers exist, which tier
> they sit in, and whether they are in use at all varies from circle to circle. It must
> be configurable per table, without touching the game logic.

The assignment as reported:

| Tier | Modifiers | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — highest** | **Gode**, **Halve**, **Vip** | Reported. All three are of equal rank |
| **2** | **Sans** | **Uncertain.** May be unable to overcall Gode, but this is unconfirmed |
| *does not exist* | ~~Stærke~~ | Not a bid at all at this table |

Note that Stærke is not merely "out of use" — it does not exist as a bid. A
configuration must therefore be able to omit a modifier entirely, not just rank it low.

**Suggested shape for the configuration:**

```yaml
modifiers:
  tiers:
    - [gode, halve, vip]    # tier 1 — highest, equal among themselves
    - [sans]                # tier 2 — unsettled whether Sans belongs here
  does_not_exist:
    - stærke
```

**Two tests that would settle the remainder at the table:**

- **Does it work in one direction only?** `9 gode` should be able to overcall
  `9 sans`, while `9 sans` should **not** be able to overcall `9 gode`. If that
  asymmetry holds, Sans sits below Gode. If it works both ways, Sans belongs in tier 1
  with the rest.
- **Count the escalations.** The number of times bidding can be overcalled on the same
  number equals the number of modifiers in the top tier. With Gode, Halve and Vip in
  tier 1, `9` can be overcalled **exactly three times** — `9 gode`, `9 halve`, `9 vip`
  in any order — after which you must go up to 10. If it happens four times, Sans
  belongs in tier 1 too.

The second test is the more useful one, because it does not require anyone to bid in a
particular way. You just count.

**A modifier is used up on that number.** Once a modifier bid has been made and beaten,
it cannot be bid again on the **same number in the same deal**:

```
Player A:  9
Player B:  9 gode      ← overcalls, new modifier on the same number
Player C:  9 vip       ← overcalls, equal rank but bid later
Player D:  9 gode      ← ILLEGAL. 9 gode has been bid and beaten this deal
Player D:  10          ← to take the bid you must go up a number
```

The restriction applies only to the deal in progress. In the next deal `9 gode` may be
bid freely again.

#### Model B — fixed ranking 1 to 5 (the source)

Whistklubben's ruleset instead describes a **total ranking**, where everything is
comparable with everything and the order of bidding counts for nothing.
The five modifiers are numbered 1 to 5, where **1 is weakest and 5 is strongest**:

```
 weakest ─────────────────────────────────────────► strongest

    1            2            3            4            5
┌─────────┐ ┌─────────┐ ┌─────────┐ ┌─────────┐ ┌─────────┐
│  drawn  │ │ Stærke  │ │  Gode   │ │  drawn  │ │  drawn  │
│         │ │(spades) │ │ (clubs) │ │         │ │         │
└─────────┘ └─────────┘ └─────────┘ └─────────┘ └─────────┘
     ▲                                    ▲           ▲
     └─── Vip, Halve and Sans go on these three ──────┘
```

Stærke and Gode are fixed at no. 2 and no. 3. The other three — Vip, Halve and Sans —
are "drawn from occasion to occasion". **Who draws them, when, and how is nowhere
stated in the source** (see [Open questions](#open-questions), no. 8).

Note that the source places **Gode as no. 3 out of 5** — there are therefore two
modifiers *above* Gode. The source does not support a reading in which Gode beats
everything else.

#### The one thing both models agree on

**A higher number always beats a lower number**, with or without a modifier. The
modifier only matters once two bids stand on the same number.

### 2.9 Påhæng (bid modifiers)

There are **five modifiers**: Vip, Stærke, Gode, Halve and Sans.

The game uses **rotating modifiers**. Stærke and Gode always hold fixed positions as
2nd and 3rd in the modifier hierarchy, while the other three are drawn afresh from
occasion to occasion:

```
1)  drawn modifier  (Vip, Halve or Sans)
2)  Stærke  (spades)        ← fixed
3)  Gode    (clubs)         ← fixed
4)  drawn modifier  (Vip, Halve or Sans)
5)  drawn modifier  (Vip, Halve or Sans)
```

#### Vip ("flip")

The trump suit is settled by **turning (flipping) the kitty cards one at a time**
until the declarer accepts the trump suit or the third card is turned.

- When Vip is bid, players who have not passed may **go along with the vip**. Bidding
  continues around the table until one or more winners emerge.
- Those in the vip take turns saying whether they want to stop, and **win the bid by
  accepting the trump suit**.
- If all three cards are flipped without anyone accepting, **the player sitting last
  wins the bid**.
- **If play stops on a joker, the hand is played without trump.**
- The declarer may exchange with the kitty.
- **The partner ace is called before the flipping begins**, by the player who was
  first to bid Vip. Precisely because the ace is called first, **trump may be the same
  suit as the partner ace**.

#### Stærke ("the strong ones")

Trump is **spades**. The declarer may exchange with the kitty.

#### Gode ("the good ones")

Trump is **clubs**. The declarer may exchange with the kitty.

#### Halve ("halves")

**The partner** chooses the trump suit — which **must differ from the suit of the
partner ace** — and it is **the partner who exchanges** with the kitty.

If the partner ace lies in the exchange pile, the declarer is **self-partnered** and
then chooses trump and exchanges themselves.

#### Sans (no-trump)

**No trump.** The declarer may exchange with the kitty.

#### Without a modifier

If you win the bid without a modifier, you must settle **both** the partner ace and
trump. The partner ace may be the same suit as trump, and it may be an ace you hold
yourself (self-partnering). Once trump and partner are settled, you get the chance to
exchange three cards with the three cards in the kitty.

### 2.10 Sol-melding bids

There are **four** sol-melding bids. **All of them are solo contracts — no partner, no trump.**

The objective is inverted relative to the numeric bids: tricks are a liability, not a
gain. This changes hand evaluation completely — aces and kings become dangerous, low
cards become valuable. And since there is no trump, you cannot trump your way clear of
a trick: the obligation to follow suit and the rank of the cards are the only things
deciding who takes it.

| Bid | Trick requirement | Open hand |
|---|---|---|
| **Sol** | May take **one** trick | No |
| **Ren sol** *(listed in the ladder as Ren sol)* 🔶 | May take **no** tricks | No |
| **Bordlægger** | May take **one** trick | **Yes** |
| **Ren bordlægger** | May take **no** tricks | **Yes** |

**Sol.** The bidder may take one trick. If they take more, **they have lost and play is
broken off** — the hand is not played out to see how bad it gets.

**Ren sol.** The bidder may take no tricks.

**Bordlægger** ("table-layer"). The bidder may take one trick and must, **after the
first lead, lay their cards face up on the table so the opponents can play against
them**.

**Ren bordlægger** ("pure table-layer"). The bidder may take no tricks and likewise
lays their cards face up after the first lead.

> **Why the bordlægger bids rank higher.** Three opponents who can see the bidder's
> entire hand can coordinate their leads and systematically force a trick onto them.
> An open-hand contract is therefore far harder than the equivalent concealed one.

**Going along on a sol-melding.** When a sol-melding is bid, players who have not yet passed may
**go along** — meaning they play **the same type of sol-melding as the first bidder**.
Several players can therefore be under the same sol-melding obligation simultaneously, each
trying to push the tricks onto the others.

**Exchange rights on a sol-melding.** The **last** player to have gone along with the bid is
the one who gets the right to exchange with the kitty. The order is thus inverted
relative to a normal exchange.

### 2.11 Trump and the partner ace

The winner of the bidding settles the trump suit and designates a partner by **naming
an ace**. The rules governing the call:

- The partner ace **may** be the same suit as trump — except under Halve, where trump
  must specifically be a *different* suit from the partner ace.
- The declarer **may take themselves as partner** by naming an ace they hold. This is
  called **selvmakker** (self-partnering).
- You may also end up **with the partner ace in the kitty**, becoming self-partnered
  without having planned it.
- **The partner is revealed only when the partner ace is played.** Until then neither
  the opponents nor necessarily the declarer knows for certain who is playing with whom.
- **The ace-partner must play the ace the first time its suit is led.** You cannot keep
  your identity hidden by holding the ace back.

### 2.12 Void in the partner ace's suit

If the winner of the bidding, **after exchanging**, holds no card in the partner ace's
suit, they must be given the chance to **nominate a card from their hand that counts as
being of the same suit as the partner ace**.

- This must be done **before the hand begins**.
- The card is placed **face down**, so the opponents cannot see it.
- It is optional — but if you decline, **the partner ace cannot be led to**.

### 2.13 Jokers

Jokers **may be played at any time**, but they have **value only when led**.

A joker led to a trick wins that trick **for certain** — even if another joker is
thrown on the same trick.

> **Corollary** (not stated directly in the ruleset, but a consequence of the two
> sentences above): a joker played to a trick you did not lead has no value and cannot
> take the trick. In sol-melding contracts this means a joker in hand is harmless as long as
> you can dispose of it as a discard — but catastrophic if you end up having to lead it.

### 2.14 Scoring

The ruleset establishes that:

- Each bid carries **a set of bonus points, triggered if the contract is made**.
- Es-makker whist is a **zero-sum game**: as many points are taken from the loser as
  are given to the winner. **The points must always sum to 0.**

The specific numeric values do **not** appear in the source document's text — see
[Open questions](#open-questions).

## Part 3 — Hjerterfri

A game in its own right, with the opposite objective to whist: you want to **avoid**
taking certain cards. Known internationally as *Hearts*.

### 3.1 Setup

- **3–7 players**, best with four.
- With four players: **13 cards each**, a standard 52-card deck **without jokers**.
- **There is no trump.**

### 3.2 Penalty points

| Card | Points |
|---|---|
| Each heart | **1** (13 in total) |
| Queen of spades | **13** |
| **Total per round** | **26** |

The queen of spades is the game's most expensive single card and accounts on its own
for half the round's total points.

### 3.3 The passing rounds

Before each round, **three cards** are passed according to a fixed pattern that changes
direction from round to round:

| Round | Direction |
|---|---|
| 1 | Three cards to the player on the **left** |
| 2 | Three cards to the player on the **right** |
| 3 | Three cards to the player **opposite** |
| 4 | **No pass** |
| 5 | The pattern begins again |

**Everyone gives and receives simultaneously.** You choose your three cards **before**
you see the ones you are given.

This is typically where the queen of spades changes hands. Passing it on is a routine
manoeuvre — but the recipient then knows where it came from.

### 3.4 Play

1. Everyone receives 13 cards (with four players).
2. Pass three cards according to the round's pattern.
3. **The holder of the two of clubs leads, and must lead precisely that card.**
4. Follow suit if you can. If you cannot, play anything — subject to the three
   restrictions below.
5. **The highest card of the led suit takes the trick** and leads to the next. There
   is no trump.
6. Count points once all 13 tricks have been played.

### 3.5 The three restrictions

1. **No penalty cards may be discarded on the first trick** — neither hearts nor the
   queen of spades, unless you hold nothing else.
2. **Hearts must be "broken" before they may be led.** Hearts are broken when a player
   discards a heart because they cannot follow suit. There is no fixed trick on which
   this happens — it depends on the cards.
3. **Exception:** if you have nothing but hearts left in hand, you may lead them even
   though they have not been broken.

### 3.6 Shooting the moon

If **one player takes all 26 points** — all thirteen hearts **and** the queen of
spades — the arithmetic is reversed: **that player scores 0, and everyone else scores
26 each.**

This is **not a variant**; it is part of the ordinary rules.

The tactical consequence is considerable: a player accumulating points early in a round
may be up to something quite different from what it looks like. The opponents must
therefore consider deliberately "spoiling" the attempt by taking a single point — one
point is better than 26.

### 3.7 End of the game

**The game ends when a player reaches 100 points.** The winner is the player with the
**fewest** points — not, that is, the one who hit the limit.

### 3.8 Other player counts

- **3 players:** remove a two from the deck so everyone gets an equal number of cards —
  typically the two of clubs, after which another two (the next-lowest club) leads.
  Agree on this beforehand.
- **5 or more:** remove correspondingly low cards. Agree in advance which ones, and who
  leads.

### 3.9 Common house rules 🔶

- **The queen of spades breaks hearts** — the queen is given the same effect as a heart.
- **The ten of diamonds scores minus 10** — adding a card you actually *want*.
  - *At the user's table it is the **jack of diamonds** that scores minus 10 — the ten never has. The source mentions only the ten.*
- **Point limits other than 100.**

## Open questions

The following is absent from the sources and has **deliberately not been filled in with
guesswork**. These must be settled before the rules can be implemented in full.

1. **The scoring table for es-makker whist.** The source document states that each bid
   carries bonus points and that the game is zero-sum — but the text does not contain
   the specific numeric values for the individual bids.
2. **The Ren sol / Ren sol naming conflict.** The source's bidding ladder lists
   *Ren sol* in position 7, while the section on sol-melding bids calls the same bid
   *Ren sol*. They are treated here as the same bid.
3. **The opening bid.** The text says numeric values run from 7 to 13, but the ladder
   as set out begins at 8. Whether 7 is a legal bid is not stated.
4. **Ace low in sol-melding.** Another source (hjerteres.dk) states that the ace ranks lowest
   in sol-melding contracts. **Whistklubben's ruleset does not mention this**, and it has
   therefore *not* been adopted here.
5. **Settlement on an aborted Sol.** It is clear that play is broken off when a Sol
   bidder takes a second trick, but not how points are computed in that situation.
6. **Who leads in es-makker whist.** The source does not explicitly state whether
   eldest hand or the declarer leads to the first trick.
7. **How the bidding ladder and the modifier hierarchy combine.** The source sets out
   each separately but never explicitly states that the `+` step expands into five
   levels ranked by the modifiers' positions. The expanded ladder in
   [2.8.1](#281-what-beats-what) is an inference, consistent with the ruleset's own
   examples but not stated verbatim in the source.
8. **Who draws the modifiers, and when.** The source says Vip, Halve and Sans are
   "drawn from occasion to occasion", but not who does it, when it happens, or whether
   it is an action at the table at all rather than a turn of phrase. The whole of
   model B rests on this single word.
9. **Which of the two models applies.** Model A (sequence decides between equal-ranked
   modifiers) and model B (fixed ranking 1–5) are irreconcilable. The question is not
   settled here, and it is the single most important decision for implementing the
   bidding logic.
10. **Sans's tier.** Gode, Halve and Vip are confirmed as equal in the top tier. Sans
    is the only unsettled one: it may sit a tier below, but this is unconfirmed. The
    two tests in [2.8.1](#281-what-beats-what) would settle it. The tier mechanic
    itself is not in doubt and holds without exception.
11. **Which modifiers exist at all.** This varies locally — Stærke, for instance, is
    not a bid at some tables. Must be configuration, not an assumption in the code.
